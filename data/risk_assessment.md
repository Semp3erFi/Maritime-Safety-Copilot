# Maritime Risk Assessment

## Overview

Risk assessment is a structured process for understanding hazards and deciding whether existing safety controls are adequate.

Within maritime safety management, the objective is not simply to complete a form. The purpose is to support informed decisions before people, the ship, cargo or the environment are exposed to unnecessary risk.

## Hazard and risk

A **hazard** is a source or situation with the potential to cause harm.

**Risk** considers both the possibility that the hazardous event will occur and the seriousness of its consequences.

A simplified concept is:

**Risk = likelihood combined with consequence severity.**

Organizations commonly use a risk matrix to categorize risk levels.

## Basic process

A practical risk-assessment process can be represented as:

1. Define the activity.
2. Identify hazards.
3. Identify possible consequences.
4. Review existing safeguards.
5. Estimate likelihood.
6. Estimate consequence severity.
7. Determine the initial or current risk level.
8. Introduce additional controls where necessary.
9. Evaluate residual risk.
10. Communicate the controls and monitor the activity.

## Examples of maritime hazards

Hazards may include:

- flammable atmospheres;
- toxic substances;
- moving machinery;
- stored pressure;
- electrical energy;
- working at height;
- slips and falls;
- heavy suspended loads;
- adverse weather;
- human fatigue;
- communication failures;
- navigational hazards;
- simultaneous operations.

## Hierarchy of controls

Where practical, stronger risk controls should be considered before relying primarily on individual behaviour.

Typical categories include:

- elimination of the hazard;
- substitution;
- engineering controls;
- administrative or procedural controls;
- personal protective equipment.

Not every maritime hazard can be eliminated. In those cases, multiple independent or complementary safeguards may be necessary.

## Residual risk

Risk should be reassessed after additional controls are introduced.

The risk that remains after controls are applied is commonly called **residual risk**.

If residual risk remains unacceptable according to the organization's criteria, the activity should not proceed without further controls or appropriate management decisions.

## Dynamic conditions

A risk assessment should not be treated as permanently valid.

Conditions may change because of:

- weather;
- equipment failure;
- changes in personnel;
- changes in cargo;
- simultaneous operations;
- unexpected hazards;
- changes in the scope of work.

Personnel should stop and reassess the activity when assumptions used in the original assessment are no longer valid.

## Relationship with the ISM Code

The ISM framework requires companies to assess identified risks to their ships, personnel and the environment and to establish appropriate safeguards.

Risk management should therefore be integrated into operational procedures and the broader Safety Management System.

## Important note

Specific risk matrices, acceptance criteria and approval levels differ between organizations. The company's SMS and applicable procedures define how risk assessment is formally conducted and authorized.
